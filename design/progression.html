How a run adds up

A proposal for what the player gains between chambers and between runs — built from what the reference games actually do, and checked against the rules we have already locked.

How to read the tags

evidenced means a shipped game does this and I can point at it. inference means it follows from our constraints but nobody has proven it for us. guard marks a rule that exists to stop a known failure, not to add anything.

01 The shape in one sentence

Your roster grows while your ground shrinks. Every chamber you clear widens what you are allowed to place; every route you take narrows where you are allowed to place it. The run is the collision of those two curves, and it ends when the board gets smaller faster than your options get better.

That is the whole pitch. It matters because it is a progression curve only a lane game can have — ground is the resource being spent, not hit points or a damage multiplier. A generic “get stronger each level” curve would work in any genre, which is exactly why it would not be memorable here.

02 Three loops, and what survives each one

Seconds · inside a chamber Gold becomes goblins Purses off the dead, gold out of the ground, goblins fused on the board. This is the part that is built and plays well. Survives: nothing. The board is wiped.
Minutes · across a run Roster against hazards Each chamber ends in a route choice that trades a worse board for a better roster. Ten chambers, about twenty minutes. Survives: what you may place, and the hazards you accepted.
Sessions · across runs The pool widens Feats you perform unlock new goblins, fusions, hazards and heroes into the pool that runs draw from. Survives: variety. Never power.
The single most important call here inference

The roster carries between chambers. The board does not. You re-place every goblin, every chamber, from a widening list.

This is PvZ's own model — you replant every level and progression lives in the seed roster — and it is right for us for a specific reason: placement is the game. If your Ballista Crew persists into the next chamber, chamber one's decisions quietly decide chambers two through ten and you stop making the choice the game is about. Carrying the board also makes fusion a one-time investment instead of something you do every chamber.

03 Between chambers: one screen, one trade

Ten chambers in twenty minutes leaves about two minutes each, so whatever sits between them has to resolve in ten or fifteen seconds. That rules out a rewards screen and a map and a shop. So collapse them: the route card is the reward. Picking where you go is picking what you get.

Route — the old workings Flooded floor

Both cards name their hazard before you commit, which is already the rule in the design doc. What is new is that the reward is on the same card, so there is exactly one decision and it is a real one: how much board am I willing to sell for this?

Reconciling “choose one of three” with your no-RNG rule guard

You have twice thrown out designs where randomness owned a counter, and you were right both times. A choice of three rewards is not that, but it can decay into it. The guard: the next hazard is named on the route card, and at least one of the three rewards always answers it. The choice stays real — take the answer, or gamble that you can already handle it and take the better long-term piece — but being locked out is structurally impossible.

04 Between runs: feats, not currency

You have already locked this to variety, milestones and no grind currency, which the research supports more strongly than I expected. Into the Breach is the cleanest case: unlocked squads and pilots “aren't upgrades, they are more like sidegrades to spice up the game and add variety.” Isaac does the same through achievements rather than a shop.

So unlocks are triggered by feats performed in a run, not by a counter that fills up: fuse a Great Wall; clear a chamber without losing a goblin; win with three lanes sealed; let the Reeve take a goblin. Each adds something to the pool — a goblin, a fusion result, a hero, a hazard, a route.

The failure this is guarding against guard

The loudest complaint about roguelite meta progression is that games get balanced around it, so early runs are “much more difficult / borderline impossible until you unlock enough stuff” — you are playing at a penalty until you have ground it off.

The rule: world one must be winnable with zero unlocks, and it must stay that way. Every balance pass gets checked against a fresh save. If that ever fails, the unlocks have become power and we cut them back.

Unlocks should make the game harder, not easier

The counter-intuitive one, and it is straight from Slay the Spire: a small pool is easy and one-dimensional, and widening it is what creates difficulty and variety. New heroes and hazards entering the pool is progression in exactly the same sense that new goblins are. That framing also solves the “does a losing run feel worth it” question in the design doc: a run that failed at chamber six but pulled off a feat still moved the game forward.

Into the Breach Achievements pay coins that unlock squads. Squads and pilots are sidegrades. One pilot carries to the next timeline. The whole meta model, plus: one thread of continuity is enough to make runs feel connected.
Hades Mirror of Night spends Darkness on abilities — and the spend is refundable. Not the currency. The reversibility: experimentation is only free if mistakes are.

Slay the Spire Card pool widens slowly across runs, deliberately diluting reliable strategies. Unlocks add difficulty and variety together. Permission to stop treating unlocks as rewards.

Plants vs. Zombies One new plant at the end of most levels. Levels 1 and 3 simple, 2 and 4 expand, 5 is a bonus. Cadence. One new idea per chamber, and a non-combat beat roughly every fifth.

06 The one carried goblin

Into the Breach lets a single pilot survive a lost timeline. It is the cheapest emotional hook in the genre: one unit with a name and a history, and permadeath that costs you something you can picture. inference

Proposal: when a run ends, one surviving goblin comes with you — keeping its name and a tally of the runs it has lived through, but none of its fusions. It starts the next run as a plain goblin who has simply been here before. No power carries; only attachment.

This is the piece I would cut first if the meta starts feeling cluttered. It is a flourish, not a system, and it earns its place only if losing that goblin later actually stings.

07 What I would prototype, in order

  • The route card. Two cards, hazard and reward on each, between the chambers that already exist. This is the load-bearing piece and it is a day of work — everything else in this document is decoration on top of it.
  • Hazards that stack. Three is enough to test: lanes sealed, columns buried, recharge lengthened. The question is whether a shrinking board stays interesting or just gets annoying, and only playing it answers that.
  • A roster that grows. Start at three goblins, end the run at six. Watch whether the sixth feels like a gift or like admin.
  • Feats and the unlock pool — last. It is the layer that needs the other two working before it can be judged, and the easiest to fake in testing.
Where this proposal is weakest

Two places I am not confident. First, ten chambers may be too many — if the roster tops out at chamber six, the last four are a victory lap, and the design doc already flags run length as untested. Six chambers with denser choices may be the better run.

Second, I have assumed hazards are interesting. A board that only ever gets worse is a slow squeeze, and slow squeezes can read as punishment rather than tension. If playtests say so, the fix is to let some routes give ground back — a collapsed lane reopened, a column cleared — so the board breathes instead of only closing.

Sources: Into the Breach and Hades progression analyses, Slay the Spire map and card-pool design notes, Binding of Saac unlock structure, and the Plants vs. Zombies Adventure Mode level breakdown. Nothing here is built yet — it is a proposal to argue with.
